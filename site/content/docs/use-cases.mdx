Spool fits when several sources name the same things, some of them only in text, and other systems store the ID. Each case shows the mess, how it maps onto Spool, and a worked example.

## Venture capital

This one is from Alex Patow, who built Spool.

I have built software for venture capital funds since 2020, and I wrote [Building for VC](https://buildingfor.vc), a guide for engineers who join a fund. I built Spool because mapping a huge number of data sources into one representation of each company, person and deal was one of the hardest problems I had there. Most datasets held some piece of the truth, and the complete picture came from several that overlapped. It was just as hard to reason about why two records had been merged, and to separate them when they should not have been.

**The mess.** A fund buys data about the same companies, people and investors from several providers, and each provider has its own IDs. Funding rounds from PitchBook, hiring from a people-data provider and the team's notes in the CRM can all name one company differently. Guess wrong and you double-count its funding, or miss that a partner already passed. Investors have it too: the CRM names the firm, one provider names its Fund II, another repeats the firm's name, and adding up the three inflates the totals.

Much of what a fund knows is text: research, meeting notes and memos, spread across documents and chat threads. A memo from last year names Acme Systems. The company is called Acme Robotics now, and whoever looks it up should find it and still see which name the memo used.

**With Spool.**

- Each provider's export is a source, and each row is a record named by source and key, so `crm:42` and `funding:42` are two records.
- A memo is a document. An extractor finds the companies, people and funds it names, and each mention keeps its character range.
- Rules on domain and name judge pairs. Each company is an identity, and its ID is what the CRM and the warehouse store.
- `spool explain` shows why two records share an identity: the rule that matched them, or who merged them and why. Undo a wrong merge, or split it, and the split keeps the sides apart from then on.
- A funding round can be an entity type too, so two feeds' entries for one Series A become one round before anyone adds up the money.

**Example.** Three rows, one from each provider, and a memo:

```text
source   key  name                website
crm      42   Acme                acme-robotics.example
funding  42   Acme Robotics Inc.  https://www.acme-robotics.example/
people   319  Acme Systems        acmesystems.example
```

```text
Passed on Acme Systems in March. Come back to them when the second pilot is signed.
```

A transform turns the website into a `domain` feature. One rule matches on it, and the sample project's rule matches close names:

```sql
-- transforms/domain_of.sql: "https://www.acme-robotics.example/" becomes "acme-robotics.example"
lambda website: nullif(regexp_extract(lower(website), '^(?:https?://)?(?:www\.)?([^/:?#]+)', 1), '')
```

```sql
-- entities/company/rules/match/same_domain.sql
l.domain = r.domain
```

After the rows and the memo are matched, `spool show records` prints:

```text
source and key                 name                identity
crm:42                         Acme                01a1f134-c68e-7020-b3af-615f8b55ad1c
funding:42                     Acme Robotics Inc.  01a1f134-c68e-7020-b3af-615f8b55ad1c
memos:memo-2026-03.txt[10-22]  Acme Systems        01a1f134-c68f-7171-b3ec-d25a61125b0f
people:319                     Acme Systems        01a1f134-c68f-7171-b3ec-d25a61125b0f
```

The CRM row and the funding row share a domain. Acme Systems has a different domain and a name too far from Acme, so it and the memo's mention form a second company. Someone who knows it's the former name merges the two, with that reason. The memo's mention now belongs to Acme Robotics, still pointing at the characters that say Acme Systems, and the merge explains itself:

```text
`people:319` and `funding:42` belong to the same identity, 01a1f134-c68e-7020-b3af-615f8b55ad1c.
Run 4 (strategy `rules`) made them a candidate pair (block rule `name_prefix`) and gave no judgement.
...
`people:319` was placed at log position 3 by `user:01a1f134-c5cc-7462-bd8e-190729dee5e1` ("Acme Systems is the former name of Acme Robotics").
```

## Sales accounts and contacts

**The mess.** Two reps added the same account as "Acorn Tools" and "Acorn Tools Ltd". Contacts arrive from email and the calendar with an address but no account. Call notes name accounts the CRM spells differently, or doesn't have. An enrichment tool adds a third spelling.

**With Spool.**

- The CRM's accounts and contacts are sources. An email or calendar export is another, with each address's domain as a feature.
- Call notes are documents, and an extractor finds the companies they name.
- Rules match accounts by domain or close name, and keep apart those in different countries. Contacts match by email.
- The account's identity ID goes in a CRM field. When two identities merge, the old ID resolves to the survivor, and the [log](/docs/using-the-ids#follow-the-log) tells every consumer.

**Example.** The sample project in [Getting started](/docs/getting-started) is this case: nine CRM rows and a call note.

```text
Call with Dana Reyes on 12 November. She has moved to Acorn Tools Ltd and runs
purchasing there. They now ship through Larkfield Logistics Ltd, and she asked
whether we work with Brightwater Foods Ltd, their new supplier.
```

Acorn Tools Ltd and Larkfield Logistics Ltd join the CRM's accounts, which keep their IDs. Brightwater Foods Ltd isn't in the CRM, so it gets its own identity: a lead, found in a note.

An email domain is a good feature until it's `gmail.com`. A transform that returns no domain for free email providers stops every Gmail contact from collapsing into one company:

```sql
-- transforms/email_domain.sql
lambda email: CASE WHEN lower(split_part(email, '@', 2)) IN ('gmail.com', 'outlook.com', 'yahoo.com')
  THEN NULL ELSE lower(split_part(email, '@', 2)) END
```

## Research and due diligence

**The mess.** The company you're researching, and the people around it, appear in news, filings, interview notes and your own lists, each spelled its own way. Another company shares its name in another country. You want every document about your company, and none about the other one.

**With Spool.**

- Each text is a document. An extractor finds the companies and people it names, and each mention keeps its document and character range.
- Mentions match against the companies you already hold as rows.
- A mention that fits two identities waits for review instead of joining either on a guess.

**Example.** The sample project has two Halden Groups, in GB and the US, kept apart by a country rule. A news item that starts "Halden Group has hired a new finance director" has no country to compare. The match holds its mention back, and `spool show review 1` says why:

```text
review item  1
kind         possible_merge
status       open
type         company
raised by    reconcile run 8

record                                source and key          now
d3f71e28-892d-1a65-6247-1ba9c8962736  notes:news-1.txt[0-12]  waits for an identity

identities they matched
  01a1f135-1685-7241-975d-3bc8639b41aa
  01a1f135-1685-7241-975d-3bc909b3db16
```

A reviewer assigns the mention to one of them, with a reason. [Review](/docs/concepts/review#decide-it) shows how.

## Support and customer records

**The mess.** The helpdesk has a requester's email and an organization an agent typed. Billing has accounts and invoice addresses. The product database has users and workspaces. "Which customers open the most tickets?" needs one customer ID across all three.

**With Spool.**

- Each export is a source of one entity type, `customer`, with features such as email domain and, where available, billing ID.
- Rules match on domain, and keep two different billing IDs apart.
- The warehouse joins tickets, invoices and usage on the identity ID. When someone merges two customers, the log records it, and old IDs still resolve.

**Example.** A billing account and two tickets, with the `email_domain` transform from the sales case:

```text
source    key  name                     email
billing   b-7  Larkfield Logistics Ltd  accounts@larkfield.example
helpdesk  t-1  Larkfield                ops@larkfield.example
helpdesk  t-2  Larkfield                sam.k@gmail.com
```

```text
source and key  name                     identity
billing:b-7     Larkfield Logistics Ltd  01a1f135-5ae0-759f-a44c-2f17261ce140
helpdesk:t-1    Larkfield                01a1f135-5ae0-759f-a44c-2f17261ce140
helpdesk:t-2    Larkfield                01a1f135-5b53-7714-bbf0-581b5bbd86d9
```

`t-1` shares the account's domain. `t-2` came from a Gmail address, so it has no domain, and a typed organization name isn't enough to match on. It keeps its own identity until someone who knows merges it.

## Compliance and KYC name matching

**The mess.** Customer names have to be screened against a list: sanctions, politically exposed persons, your own blocklist. Lists write names surname first, in capitals, with or without a middle name. A person checks every hit, and "we checked, this isn't the listed person" has to be recorded with who decided and why.

**With Spool.**

- Customers and the list are two sources of one entity type, `person`.
- A transform sorts a name's words, so "HOLT, Maren" and "Maren Holt" compare equal.
- A rule matches on name, and a no_match rule separates different dates of birth.
- A customer who joins an identity with a list entry is a hit. One who fits two entries waits for review.
- The analyst's decision is a changeset with an author and a reason. A `cannot_link` between a customer and an entry means no later changeset can put them together.

**Example.** The transform and the rules:

```sql
-- transforms/name_words.sql
lambda name: array_to_string(list_sort(string_split(trim(regexp_replace(lower(name), '[^a-z]+', ' ', 'g')), ' ')), ' ')
```

```sql
-- entities/person/rules/match/same_name.sql
l.name_words = r.name_words
```

```sql
-- entities/person/rules/no_match/different_birth_date.sql
l.born <> r.born
```

The list has two entries for HOLT, Maren, born in 1971 and 1980. One customer arrives with a date of birth, and the next day another arrives without one:

```text
source and key  name         identity
customers:k-1   Maren Holt   01a1f136-777c-7294-a038-849ea835b87f
customers:k-2   Maren Holt   -
list:L-88       HOLT, Maren  01a1f136-777c-7294-a038-849ea835b87f
list:L-90       HOLT, Maren  01a1f136-777c-7294-a038-849dbed83c87
```

`k-1` has `L-88`'s date of birth and joined it: a hit. `k-2` matched both entries, so it waits in a `possible_merge` item. Without a date of birth, picking either would be a guess.

## Agent memory and knowledge bases

**The mess.** An agent remembers what it reads and hears: conversations, notes, wiki pages, tickets. "Dana", "Dana Reyes" and "the purchasing lead at Acorn" are one person, and "Acorn" and "ACORN TOOLS" are one company. A memory that stores each as written fills with duplicates. One that merges by guesswork mixes up two people's facts, and nobody can say why later.

**With Spool.**

- What the agent reads is stored as documents, and an extractor finds what they name, and where.
- Mentions resolve to the same identities as your lists' rows. The identity ID is the key the memory files facts under, or the tag on a retrieved chunk.
- An agent connected to [`/mcp`](/docs/mcp) looks up identities, asks why two records matched with `explain_pair`, and fixes a wrong match with a merge, split or undo. Each correction is a changeset with an author and a reason.

**Example.** In the sample project, an agent asked which notes mention Acorn Tools needs two tools. `search_identities` finds the identity by a word in its records, and `get_identity` lists its five records. One is `notes:notes-1.txt[54-69]`: the note, and the characters that say "Acorn Tools Ltd".

## Catalogs

**The mess.** Several suppliers list the same songs, or products, each their own way. One writes the album into the title, another puts the artist first. A length is "02:52" in one and "2.87" minutes in another, and a year is "1972" or "'72".

**With Spool.**

- Each supplier is a source, with its own transforms that turn its columns into shared features: title, artist, album, track number, seconds, year.
- Rules compare the features, and a product's ID survives next month's feed.

[Entity types and features](/docs/concepts/entity-types-and-features) builds a two-supplier product catalog from scratch.

**Example.** [`benchmarks/resolution/music`](https://github.com/alexpatow/spool/tree/main/benchmarks/resolution/music) does this for MusicBrainz songs across five sources. These three rows from its sample are one song:

```text
title                                                        length   artist                         album                                   year
Moonlight Serenade (The Persuasive Trombone of Urbie Green)  02:52    Urbie Green And His Orchestra  The Persuasive Trombone of Urbie Green  1972
Urbie Green And His Orchestra - Moonlight Serenade           unknown                                 The Persuasive Trombone of Urbie Green  72
Moonlight Serenade - The Persuasive Trombone of Urbie Green  2.87     Urbie Green And His Orchestra                                          '72
```

On 193,750 records, its rules run in 8 seconds on four cores, at pair precision 0.997 and recall 0.862. Its first rules, "four of the six values agree", scored 0.998 precision on 19,375 records but 0.157 on 1,937,500, where 2,532 different songs called "Intro" at track 1 collapsed into one identity. Measure rules at the size they'll run at.
